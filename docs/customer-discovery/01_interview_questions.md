# BiomeScan B2B and B2G interview questions

Adapted for the BIOF4012 student team on 3 November 2026. Use with the separate interviewer guidelines and investigator-confirmed participant information and consent. Keep the selected problem topic consistent throughout the interview.

Choose one task before starting: invasive-plant inspection and management; farm weed inspection and management; or standing-water and mosquito-prevention inspection. Recruit a person with direct work experience in that task. Complete the agreed consent procedure before asking research questions.

Ask the eight core questions and up to four optional questions within 15–20 minutes. Probe one recent example rather than rushing through every prompt. The five themes preserve the original course sample. Introduce the solution only in the optional closing stage on the final page.

## Theme one The workflow walkthrough

Preserves the sample's request to start with the last real event. Do not begin by asking whether the participant needs better technology.

### Question 1 Core

“Walk me through the most recent time your team carried out [selected task]. What triggered the work, and what happened from the first check until it was considered complete?”

If needed, ask who performed each step and who decided the task was finished. Let the participant choose the example. If they have no direct experience, establish the limits of their role rather than treating a hypothetical answer as evidence.

### Question 2 Core

“How did you decide where to check, record what you found, and follow up any outstanding items?”

If useful, ask what records, maps or checklists already exist and how they are used. Ask about satisfactory parts of the process as well as gaps. Do not assume the organization lacks a map or follow-up system.

### Question 3 Optional

“For that example, roughly how much area or how many inspection locations were involved, how many people took part, and how much time did the work take?”

Use ranges if preferred. Separate inspection, identification, clearance/treatment, reporting and repeat visits. Establish frequency and seasonality only as needed. Do not multiply uncertain estimates into an apparent market size.

## Theme two The frustration inventory

Adapt the sample to allow an answer that the current workflow is adequate.

### Question 4 Core

“What, if anything, made that job difficult, delayed it or led to extra work? What happened as a result?”

Ask for a specific example or consequence: staff time, a repeat visit, delayed closure or an unresolved finding. Do not introduce fines, disease or safety incidents unless the participant raises them. Ask what worked well too.

### Question 5 Core

“What have you already tried to improve that part of the process, and what happened?”

If nothing has been tried, ask what has kept the organization from acting. A satisfactory current process, low priority or lack of funds are useful findings. Compare with existing field checks, contractors and record systems only after the participant describes them.



## Theme three The alternative history

Ask for a useful outcome and management decision rather than a feature wishlist.

### Question 6 Core

“If you could change one part of this process, which would you choose? How would you know the change had been worthwhile?”

Let the participant define success before offering examples. Accept that no change may be needed.

### Question 7 Optional

“What decision, if any, is currently difficult because the available information is insufficient or uncertain? How do you make that decision today?”

Distinguish missing information from insufficient staff, access or treatment capacity.

## Theme four The priority tradeoff

Compare stated importance with recent action and resource allocations.

### Question 8 Core

“Compared with your other operational responsibilities, where does this issue sit? What has your team chosen to deal with first?”

If using a 1–10 score, ask what explains it and what action followed.

### Question 9 Optional

“What resources are currently allocated to this task, and what happens when that is not enough?”

Accept broad ranges or a refusal. Separate current fixed-price spending from funds for additional work. Do not show the pitch's prices or ask willingness to pay for an undisclosed product.

## Theme five The social and organizational context

Distinguish the user, action owner, approver and payer.

### Question 10 Core

“Who uses the findings, who decides what action to take, and who could approve resources to change the process?”

Role names are sufficient. Technical interest does not establish purchasing authority.

### Question 11 Core

“When your organization last commissioned a comparable inspection or specialist service, how was it specified, approved and paid for?”

Ask which role knows. Probe company/project budgets or the applicable public purchasing route and required evidence. Avoid non-public tender information or purchase commitments.

### Question 12 Optional

“Which other role would give us a different view of this workflow? If appropriate, would you be comfortable forwarding our interview invitation?”

Let them forward the invitation. A referral establishes access, not demand.

## Optional concept discussion at the very end

Finish the problem and purchasing questions first. Ask whether the participant has anything to add, then say: “That completes our questions about the current process. If you are interested, may I take the final minute to explain the idea behind the project?” Omit this stage if time is short or its inclusion is outside the study scope confirmed by the investigators.

Choose only the relevant description. For farm interviews use the invasive-plant description, referring to the weeds the participant actually identified; do not assume Mikania is present:

**Invasive plants:** “Our team is exploring whether drone-based hyperspectral surveys, checked against field observations, could help locate invasive plants and assess change after management. We have a research demonstrator; field performance and commercial usefulness still need testing.”

**Standing water:** “Our team is exploring whether aerial imaging, including hyperspectral sensing, could help prioritize checks of visible standing water. This is an unvalidated concept. It would not establish mosquito presence, find every concealed source or replace necessary field checks.”

Ask one neutral reaction: “Where would this fit poorly with the process you described, and what evidence would you need before considering it further?” Keep this reaction separate from the unprompted problem evidence. Do not introduce a demo, price or sales negotiation into the remaining minute.

Thank them. If appropriate, ask separately whether they are willing to receive one further contact. Research consent does not itself permit ongoing marketing, a pilot, site access or reuse of identifiable interview data in a commercial proposal.
